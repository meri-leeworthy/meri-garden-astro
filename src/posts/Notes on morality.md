The purpose of a lot of writing is moral instruction, and the dynamic between writer and reader is in this case something a bit like parent and child. Children (and many adults) require moral instruction on their path to developing an internal ethical compass. The parent provides guidelines, rules, boundaries which hopefully serve to model how the child's relationships with others will generally work. 

These models in time come to structure all of our social behaviours and many of our conscious thoughts, whether they are communicated in the form of physical punishment, reward, non-verbal communication, spoken injunctions, written rules, essays, or even works of art, and they do this whether or not these models are coherent and consistent with each other. Their purpose is to provide a scaffold for the development of autonomous social being. Such scaffolding is an essential part of human reproduction, once the biological-genetic part has been handled. 

It is also, of course, inherently political and contested, so it makes sense that moral instruction constitutes such a significant proportion of all political communication. The capacity to perform moral instruction is a significant domain of power vis-à-vis the reproduction of social life, and this lens is helpful in analysing the politics of public education as well as evangelical religions, popular culture, 



- sociality is fundamentally moral and emotional - this developmentally (and epistemologically) precedes 'rationality', the symbolic domain, formal logic, 'instrumental reason' independent of or abstracted from the relational nature of being
- morality as dialectical, relational foundation of ontology
- markets, private property as moral institutions 

Given the spectrum of moral practices ranging from physical punishment to supportive, caring pedagogy and modelling, what is it that makes the former, domination-driven practices so effective and popular in the reproduction of sociality? Here the notion of 'generational trauma' is often invoked, affecting (in its inverse, generational healing) an ethic of relationality, of respect for autonomy at the social scale.
